To make a scanned PDF searchable, run it through OCR (optical character recognition), which reads the text in each page image and adds it to the file as real text. Upload your document to our <a href="/ocr-pdf">OCR PDF</a> tool and download the searchable version. The pages look exactly the same, but Ctrl+F and copy-paste now work.

If you've ever tried to search a scanned contract and got zero results, this is why. To your computer, a scan is a photo of a page, with no words in it at all.

<h2>How to Tell If Your PDF Is Scanned</h2>

Open the file and try two quick tests:

- Try to highlight a sentence with your cursor. If you get a blue box around the whole page, or nothing happens, it's an image
- Press Ctrl+F and search for a word you can see on the page. No results means there's no text layer

Some files are mixed, with typed pages and scanned pages together. A contract with a scanned signature page is a common example.

<h2>What OCR Does to Your File</h2>

OCR looks at each page image, recognises the letters and places the matching text in an invisible layer exactly on top of the words on the page. Nothing you can see changes. Your stamps, signatures and layout all stay the same, but you can now select, search and copy the text.

Our tool checks each page first. Pages that already have real text are left alone, and only scanned pages are processed, so mixed PDFs work without any extra steps.

<h2>How to Get Accurate Results</h2>

OCR is only as good as the image it reads. A few small things make a big difference:

- Scan at 300 dpi. Below about 200 dpi, small letters start to blur together
- Fix sideways pages first. Use <a href="/rotate-pdf">Rotate PDF</a> so that text reads upright, because OCR can't read a page that's turned on its side
- Keep the page flat and straight. Curved lines near a book's spine and tilted phone photos cause the most errors
- Use good, even light. Shadows from your hand or phone can hide whole words
- Don't compress before OCR. Heavily compressed scans lose the detail OCR needs, so run OCR first and then use <a href="/compress-pdf">Compress PDF</a> afterwards if you need a smaller file

Expect very good results on clean printed documents, and weaker results on faded photocopies, unusual fonts and tables. Handwriting usually doesn't come out well.

<h2>What You Can Do with a Searchable PDF</h2>

Once the text layer is there, a scan becomes much more useful:

- Find a clause in a long contract in seconds instead of reading through it
- Copy a paragraph into an email or report without retyping it
- Search your folders for documents by their contents, on systems that index PDF text
- Use a screen reader, which can't read a plain image of a page
- Pull out figures and paste them into a spreadsheet

Always proofread anything important. OCR can confuse similar characters, such as 0 and O or 1 and l, and that matters in names, amounts and account numbers.

<h2>Is OCR the Same as Converting a PDF to Word?</h2>
Not quite. OCR adds a text layer and keeps the PDF looking the same. Converting to Word gives you an editable document, but the layout of a scan may shift. If you want to keep the look of the page and just search it, a searchable PDF is the better choice.

<h2>Will OCR Change How My PDF Looks?</h2>
No. The text layer is invisible, and the page images stay as they are. The file may get a little bigger because of the added text.

<h2>Can OCR Read Handwriting?</h2>
Not reliably. OCR is built for printed text, so neat block capitals might partly work, but joined-up handwriting usually won't. If handwriting matters, plan to check or retype it yourself.
