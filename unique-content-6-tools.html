Combine PDFs without losing a single detail

When you merge two or more PDFs here, the original files are stitched together page-by-page exactly as they were created. Fonts stay embedded, vector text stays selectable, hyperlinks keep working, and form fields don't flatten into images. A lot of "free PDF mergers" re-render every page to a picture to make the job easier on their servers — that's why your text suddenly looks blurry and you can no longer copy from it. We don't do that. Your 12-page contract plus a 3-page appendix comes out as a clean 15-page document that behaves like one file.

Order matters when you combine documents, so you set it before merging. Drag the thumbnails into the sequence you want, and the final PDF follows that exact order — cover letter first, resume second, references last, no surprises. There's no cap that forces you to merge in small batches, and you're not asked to log in or hand over an email just to download the result.

Where merging actually saves you time

  • Month-end invoicing: roll 20 separate vendor invoices into one PDF your accountant can flip through in order instead of opening 20 attachments.
  • Job applications: some portals allow a single upload — combine resume, cover letter and portfolio so nothing gets dropped.
  • Scanned paperwork: a scanner that saves one PDF per page leaves you with a mess; merge them back into the original multi-page document.
  • Court and case files: assemble exhibits, filings and correspondence into a single indexed bundle.
  • Study packs: join lecture notes, past papers and your own summaries into one revision file you can search end to end.

Pull out exactly the pages you need

Splitting a PDF is the opposite problem to merging, and it shows up just as often. Maybe a 60-page report has one section you need to send, or a scanned bank statement has three months bundled into one file and you only want March. Here you pick the page range — or several ranges — and download just those pages as a fresh PDF, while the original stays untouched. You can also break a document into single pages if you need each one as its own file.

Because we extract pages rather than re-printing them, the pages you pull out keep their original text layer, bookmarks within range, and image quality. That matters when the pages will be read, searched or printed later — a split that secretly turns your text into a flat image looks fine on screen and then fails the moment someone tries to copy a line from it.

Common reasons to split a PDF

  • Send less, not more: share only the relevant chapter of a long manual instead of forcing someone to scroll past 50 pages.
  • Separate scanned batches: a single scan of mixed documents (ID, lease, payslip) can be split back into individual files.
  • Privacy: remove pages with sensitive details before forwarding a statement or report.
  • File-size limits: break an oversized PDF into smaller parts that slip under email or upload caps.
  • Print what you need: extract just the pages you'll actually print to save paper and ink.

Make a PDF small enough to actually send

Most oversized PDFs are heavy for one reason: high-resolution images embedded at far more detail than the screen or printer will ever use. Our compressor targets that waste first — it downsamples and re-encodes images intelligently while leaving your text as sharp vector data. That's how a 40 MB scanned brochure can drop to a few megabytes without turning into a pixelated mess. You choose how aggressive to be: a lighter setting for documents you'll print, a stronger setting for files you just need to email or upload.

The point of compression isn't a smaller number for its own sake — it's getting under a limit. Email attachments usually cap around 25 MB, government and job portals are often stricter, and messaging apps choke on big files. Shrinking the PDF here means the document still opens and reads the same; it just travels without bouncing back.

When you'll reach for compression

  • Email bounce-backs: that "message exceeds size limit" reply usually disappears after one pass.
  • Upload forms: visa, university and tender portals frequently demand files under 2–5 MB.
  • Scanned documents: scans are image-heavy by nature and compress dramatically.
  • Storage and backups: a folder of fat PDFs adds up fast on cloud drives.
  • Faster sharing: smaller files upload and download in a fraction of the time on slow connections.

Compress PDF — questions answered

How much smaller will my file get?

It depends on what's inside. Image-heavy and scanned PDFs often shrink by 70–90%; files that are mostly plain text are already small, so the gain is more modest.

Will compressing ruin the quality?

On the recommended setting the difference is hard to spot on screen and in normal printing. If you need print-perfect output, pick the lighter compression level for a smaller-but-safer reduction.

Can I still edit or copy text after compressing?

Yes. We don't flatten text into an image, so the compressed PDF stays selectable, searchable and editable in your usual PDF tools.

Is compression reversible?

Compression itself isn't undone, but your original is never overwritten — keep it, and you can always re-compress at a different level if you want.

Turn a PDF back into an editable Word document

A PDF is built to look the same everywhere, which is exactly why it's annoying to edit. Converting to Word (.docx) rebuilds the document as real, editable text — not a picture of text — so you can fix a typo, update a figure or change a clause without retyping the whole thing. Our converter works to preserve the structure that matters: paragraphs, headings, bullet lists, tables and basic styling come across so you spend your time editing, not rebuilding the layout.

Two kinds of PDF behave differently, and it's worth knowing which you have. A PDF that was exported from Word or another program already contains real text and converts cleanly. A PDF that's a scan or photo of a page is just an image — for those, our OCR reads the characters and turns them into editable text, though heavily stylised or low-quality scans may need a quick proofread afterward.

Why people convert PDF to Word

  • Update old documents: edit a contract, policy or CV you only have as a PDF.
  • Reuse content: lift paragraphs or tables into a new document instead of copying line by line.
  • Fill in forms: turn a flat PDF form into something you can type directly into.
  • Translate or rewrite: get the text into Word where your editing and language tools live.
  • Fix formatting: reflow text that was locked into an awkward PDF layout.

PDF to Word — quick answers

Will the formatting stay the same?

For text-based PDFs, layout, tables and styles transfer closely. Very complex designs (multi-column magazines, heavy graphics) may shift slightly and need minor touch-ups in Word.

Can it convert a scanned PDF?

Yes. Scanned or image-only PDFs are run through OCR so the text becomes editable. Cleaner scans give cleaner results.

Do I get a .doc or .docx file?

You get a modern .docx file that opens in Microsoft Word, Google Docs, LibreOffice and Apple Pages.

Is there a watermark on the converted file?

No. The Word document you download is clean — no watermark stamped across your content.
